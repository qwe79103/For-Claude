House Tour Phrasebook

What to say, what to check, and how to leave without committing to anything.

Your 3:30 is well-timed. Use it.

The tour itself will take 20–30 minutes. The valuable part is what happens on either side of it — rush hour builds from about 4:00, so arriving early and leaving late gives you the noise answer for free.

  • 3:10
    Arrive early. Stay in the car.Window down, engine off. Just listen for ten minutes. This is your baseline.
  • 3:25
    Walk the block once.Look at the neighbours' houses, the street parking, where the bins go, how close the traffic actually is.
  • 3:30
    The tour.Phrases below. Film as you walk.
  • 4:05
    Ask for a few minutes alone in the main bedroom.Open the window. Listen. Close it. Listen again. That single test answers your biggest question.
  • 4:20
    Stand outside once more before you drive off.Traffic is heavier now than when you arrived. If it still feels fine, it's fine.

Bring

  • Your phone charger — plug it into an outlet in each room to check they work
  • A decibel meter app — outdoors, ~55 dB is quiet, 65+ is arterial
  • A compass app — to see which way the windows face
  • Your documents, in case the credit conversation happens: passport, DS‑2019, appointment letter, bank statement

Opening

When you arrive

"Hi, I'm Po-Hsun. Thanks for making the time."

First names are normal in the US, and using theirs back is friendly, not rude.

Setting expectations early

"I'm still looking at a few places, so I'm just getting a feel for it today."

Say this at the start. It makes it completely natural to leave without applying.

Permission to document

"Do you mind if I take some photos and a short video? I want to look at them again later."

Almost always yes. Film every room while narrating quietly — you will forget details, and it records any existing damage.

Asking to test things

Americans expect renters to test things. None of this is rude — it reads as careful.

Water

"May I run the water for a second?"

"How long does the hot water usually take?"

Turn on the kitchen tap and a shower at the same time and watch the pressure drop.

Heating — it is nearly October

"Could we turn the heat on for a minute, just to check it works?"

"How old is the furnace?"

The manufacture date is printed on a label on the furnace and the water heater. Photograph both labels.

Windows and noise

"Can I open this window?"

"Is it noisy here in the morning, around eight?"

Ask it as a neutral question, not a worried one. You'll get a more honest answer.

Signal — you'll be calling Taiwan

"Sorry, I'm just checking the phone signal — I call my family overseas a lot."

Check the basement and the main bedroom especially. Also ask which internet providers serve the house.

Money and terms

The real number

"Is $3,900 the full monthly cost, or are there other required fees on top?"

"Which utilities am I responsible for?"

Ask for a typical winter gas bill. Denver winters are cold and heating a whole house is not cheap.

Up front

"What would I need to pay before moving in, in total?"

"How much is the security deposit?"

Colorado caps the security deposit at one month's rent — 1.5 if furnished. If they say two months, that's worth questioning politely.

Length

"Would you consider a longer lease — say eighteen or twenty-four months?"

"Is the rent fixed for the whole term?"

A long lease is genuinely valuable to a landlord. It's your best bargaining chip and it costs you nothing, since you're staying two years.

Responsibilities

"Who handles the lawn and the snow?"

"Who do I call if something breaks?"

Denver requires sidewalks to be cleared within 24 hours of a snowfall. Find out whose job that is before you sign, not after.

The credit conversation

If it comes up, say it plainly and without apology. Your position is strong — you just have to frame it correctly.

The core line

"I should mention — I don't have a US credit score yet. Not a low score; I have no credit file at all, because I've never held credit in the US. I'm arriving from Taiwan on a research appointment."

The distinction between no file and bad credit is the whole argument. Make it explicitly.

What you can offer instead

"I can show proof of funds covering the whole lease, my appointment letter, and my bank statements. I'm also open to paying several months up front if that helps."

You can demonstrate liquid assets worth many times the annual rent. That is a stronger answer to "will this person pay?" than any credit score.

Giving them an easy exit

"If that doesn't work with your screening policy, I completely understand — just let me know."

Counter-intuitively, this makes people more willing to help, not less.

A fair thing to ask for

"Could you send me your written screening criteria?"

Colorado landlords are required to have a written screening policy. Asking is normal, and the answer tells you whether 650 is a hard rule or a starting point.

When you don't understand

These are the most useful phrases on this page. Native speakers talk fast and use slang. Asking someone to repeat themselves is completely normal and nobody minds.

Use freely

"Sorry, could you say that again a bit more slowly?"

"What does ___ mean?"

"Let me make sure I understood — you're saying ___?"

"Could you write that down for me?"

"Let me make sure I understood" is the strongest one. It makes you sound careful rather than confused, and it catches misunderstandings before they cost you money.

Words you'll hear

utilities
Gas, electricity, water, trash. "Utilities included" means they're in the rent.
HVAC
The heating and air conditioning system as a whole.
furnace
The gas heater. Denver houses almost all have one.
garbage disposal
The grinder in the kitchen sink drain.
security deposit
Refundable money held against damage. Capped at one month in Colorado.
application fee
Non-refundable fee to run your background check.
co-signer / guarantor
Someone who promises to pay if you don't.
lease term
How many months the lease runs.
month-to-month
No fixed end date; either side can end it with notice. Usually costs more.
walk-through
The inspection you do together at move-in and move-out. Insist on one.
as-is
They won't repair it before you move in.
renter's insurance
Cheap insurance most landlords require. About $15–25 a month.
break the lease
Leave before the end date. Ask what the penalty is.

While you walk — look for these

  • Water stains on ceilingsEspecially under bathrooms and in the basement. Brown rings mean a leak, past or present.
  • A musty smellDenver is dry. Mustiness means moisture, and moisture means a problem.
  • Windows that open, close and lockTry several. Painted-shut windows are a bad sign about upkeep.
  • Water pressure and hot water timingTwo taps at once.
  • OutletsYour charger, one per room.
  • Which way the main rooms faceWest gets the mountains and the hot afternoon sun. East gets the morning.
  • Where you'd park in snowAnd who shovels the walk.
  • Phone signal in every room
  • Any existing damagePhotograph it all today. It protects your deposit in two years.

Leaving without committing

Expect this

Agents often push for an application on the spot — "there's a lot of interest," "I have another showing tonight." Sometimes true, often just pressure. Do not sign or pay anything today. A good property will still be there tomorrow, and if it isn't, another one will be.

The polite exit

"Thank you — this is helpful. I'd like to think it over tonight and get back to you tomorrow."

"I have one more place to see before I decide."

Both are complete answers. You don't owe an explanation, and adding one weakens it.

If they push

"I understand. I'm not comfortable applying before I've read the lease, but I'll let you know tomorrow morning."

Calm and final. Reasonable people accept this immediately; the ones who don't have told you something useful.

Before you go

"Could you email me the lease so I can read it properly?"

"What's the best way to reach you?"

Getting the lease in advance is the single most useful thing you can leave with.

One last thing: if it feels wrong when you're standing there, that's real information. You are choosing where you'll live for two years, and you have options — Denver is a renter's market right now.
