The short answer

Rent it. Don't buy it.

Nothing that changed since we last talked changed the fundamental problem, and one thing that changed made it slightly worse.

Your instinct that you need five years to make money on this house is correct — and it's the reason not to buy. You are planning to be in Denver for two. Buying and selling after two years needs Denver prices to rise about 4.4% a year just to beat renting and leaving your money in T‑bills. Holding five years — living in it two, renting it out from Taiwan for three — needs about 3.0% a year to break even.

Denver's own forecasts for 2026–27 are 2–4% a year for detached houses. This is not a detached house. Attached homes in Denver — duplexes, townhomes, condos — are down roughly 2.9% over the past year, sitting on 4.8 months of supply. You would be taking a coin‑flip bet, with a small win and a large loss, to acquire a three‑year unpaid job managing a house eleven time zones away.

Meanwhile Denver is the best renter's market in sixteen years. That is the side of the table you should be on.

If you want the house itself

Rent this house.

The seller is already saying he'll lease it if it doesn't sell. Ask Karl to open that conversation now, and ask for a right of first refusal or a purchase option written into the lease. You get the house you like, at roughly $2,900 a month, with no transaction cost, no exit risk, no roof, and the option to buy it in 2027 if you decide to stay longer than two years.

Visa and foreign‑ownership rules: no obstacle

You asked whether the rules changed. For buying, they have not. There is no citizenship, green card, or visa requirement to purchase a US home. Colorado's foreign‑ownership statute (HB24‑1029) covers agricultural land, mineral rights and water rights, and names China, Russia and state sponsors of terrorism — not residential property, and not Taiwan. The bills moving in Congress in 2026 target adversary states and entities, not individual Taiwanese buyers.

What did change is how long you're allowed to stay: from 15 September 2026, DHS replaces "duration of status" for F‑1, J‑1 and I visa holders with fixed admission periods (four years for exchange visitors), with extensions filed rather than automatic. That matters for planning your two years — and it matters a great deal for the tax point further down — but it does not touch your ability to own the house.

Assumptions behind these numbers

Same starting cash in both scenarios ($623,000), with all housing costs paid out of it. Buying costs $3,000 (cash purchase, no appraisal or lender fees; Colorado sellers customarily pay the owner's title policy). Selling costs 7% of price. Rent starts at $2,900/month and grows 3%/year. Carrying costs as tabulated, with the sub‑association assumed at zero — if it turns out to be $250/month, subtract about $15,000 from the five‑year column.

The five‑year column credits three years of net rental income at roughly $13,900/year (gross rent less 8% management, a leasing fee, 5% vacancy and all carrying costs) and deducts depreciation recapture of about $13,100 at sale. It does not deduct US or Taiwanese income tax on rental profit, because depreciation should shelter most of it — nor does it price a hail claim, an extended vacancy, or a bad tenant.

Every one of those omissions cuts against buying.

The reframe

Stop negotiating toward $585,000 because that's the number your spreadsheet needs.

Negotiating only makes you money if you buy below market value — because you also sell at market. If the house is genuinely worth $605,000 and you pay $585,000, you've banked $20,000. If it's worth $585,000 and you pay $585,000, you've captured nothing; you've paid a fair price for an asset that still has to appreciate 3% a year for five years to beat a Treasury bill.

So the number to ask Karl for is not "how low will he go." It's closed comps — Central Park paired homes, 1,500–1,800 sq ft, sold in the last 90 days. That tells you what you're actually buying.

The rental option

Is $2,900 a reasonable rent? Yes — and that's the tell

Comparable Central Park three‑bedroom townhomes are currently listing between $2,695 and $3,350. For 1,639 sq ft with a two‑car garage backing onto Bluff Lake, roughly $2,800–3,100 is fair. Don't pay above $3,000.

At $2,900, the price‑to‑rent ratio is about 17.8. Anything much above 15 tilts toward renting for short horizons — and your horizon is short. The rent is reasonable in the sense that matters: it is cheap relative to what the house costs to own.

You also have the market behind you. Metro Denver apartment vacancy hit 7.6% at the end of 2025, the highest in sixteen years, average rents fell back to 2022 levels, and Denver leads the country in concessions. Single‑family rentals are tighter than apartments, but landlords are still negotiating.

Locking it in from Taiwan before October

Your worry — that someone else takes it first — is real but manageable, and smaller than it feels. Central Park has roughly 30 single‑family rentals listed at any given time; this is not a scarce commodity.

  • Start now, not in October. A landlord will hold for a qualified tenant five or six weeks out. Have Karl approach this seller directly about a lease starting 1 October.
  • A big deposit is not the lever you think. Colorado caps security deposits at one month's rent (1.5 if furnished), and as of 1 January 2026 HB25‑1249 treats deposits as tenant property held in trust. Offering three months won't be accepted.
  • What does work without US credit history: proof of funds, your employment or program letter, Taiwanese bank statements, a US‑based guarantor, or a third‑party guarantor service. Prepaid rent is sometimes structured lawfully — ask the landlord's agent, don't assume.
  • Ask for the purchase option. A right of first refusal costs the landlord nothing and keeps the door open. If you love the house in mid‑2027, buy it then — with a year of knowing the road noise, the neighbours, and whether you're staying.
The two-year problem

What the five-year hold actually asks of you

You identified this yourself and you were right to. If you buy and need five years, three of those years are spent as a landlord in Colorado while living in Taiwan. Concretely:

  • A property manager, not optional. 8–10% of rent plus a leasing fee per new tenancy. At an eleven‑hour time difference you cannot self‑manage a burst pipe.
  • A Denver rental licence. Required for any rental of 30 days or more — four‑year term, $50 fee, and a passing inspection by a certified private inspector that you arrange and pay for. The licence number must appear in every advertisement.
  • Hail. Colorado premiums are up 61% since 2023, wind/hail carries a separate 1–2% deductible ($6,000–12,000 out of pocket), and insurers increasingly pay actual cash value rather than replacement cost on older roofs. This house's roof is fourteen years old unless it's been replaced — find out which.
  • A US tax return every year. Rental income to a non‑resident is withheld at 30% of gross by default. The fix is the §871(d) election to be taxed on net income — the difference between tax on $34,800 and tax on roughly zero after depreciation. It requires filing Form 1040NR. Don't discover this in year three.
  • Roughly $100,000 frozen at closing. Selling as a non‑resident triggers FIRPTA withholding of 15% of the gross price (10% if the buyer will occupy and the price is under $1M) plus Colorado's 2% non‑resident withholding. Both are refundable when you file — months later.

None of this is fatal. All of it is unpaid work, in a second language, across a twelve‑hour gap, for an expected return the table above says is roughly zero.

The risk nobody mentions

A non‑resident gets a $60,000 US estate tax exemption — not $13 million.

If you own this house and die while still a non‑resident, the US taxes it above $60,000 at rates up to 40%. On a $620,000 house that's roughly $165,000, and there is no US–Taiwan estate tax treaty to soften it. The cheap fix is a ten‑year term life policy covering the holding period — a few hundred dollars a year for a healthy adult. (A mortgage would also reduce the US‑situs value, which is one genuine argument against paying all cash — though at 6.4% borrowing against 3.6% T‑bills, the carry costs more than the exposure.)

Highest-value item in this memo

Sell your Taiwanese stocks before 31 December 2026

This is worth more than anything you'll win in the price negotiation, and it is time‑sensitive.

Taiwan does not tax individual capital gains on listed domestic shares — you pay the 0.3% transaction tax and nothing more. The United States taxes its residents on worldwide income, including Taiwanese stock gains, at 15–20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax. Critically, there is no step‑up in basis on the day you arrive: the US would measure the gain from your original purchase price, however many years ago that was.

Arriving around October 2026 gives you roughly 90 days of US presence this year — below the substantial‑presence threshold. You should be a non‑resident for US tax purposes for all of 2026, with residency beginning in 2027. Which means the window to realise those gains outside the US tax net closes on 31 December 2026.

On a large embedded gain this is a five‑figure difference and quite possibly six. It applies whether or not you buy the house — you're moving the money either way.

Two caveats. If you'll be on an F‑1 or J‑1 visa you're an "exempt individual" (five years and two years respectively) and remain a non‑resident regardless, so the urgency drops — but confirm rather than assume, especially given the September 2026 rule change. And confirm the whole thing with a cross‑border CPA before you sell anything. This is the one item in this memo worth paying for advice on.

Moving the money

  • Taiwan raised the individual annual outward remittance cap to US$10 million in October 2024. Your $600,000 is comfortably inside it.
  • Any single remittance of US$500,000 or more requires the Foreign Exchange Receipts or Payments Declaration Form with documented source and purpose, submitted through your bank to the Central Bank. Allow two to four weeks — do not start this under a contract deadline.
  • USD/TWD is around 31.89, with the TWD down 4.2% over twelve months. You'd be converting at a relatively weak TWD. A 3% move on $600,000 is $18,000, so consider staging the conversion across two or three tranches rather than one.
  • Worth naming: a $600,000 US house is a large unhedged USD position for someone whose life may well return to Taiwan.
Your other two worries

The road, and the roof

Noise — a smaller problem than you fear, and a bigger one than Karl says

One quiet night with the windows closed, reported by the agent who wants the sale, is weak evidence. It isn't dishonest — it's just not a measurement. Do this instead:

  • Stand there on a weekday from 7:30–8:30am and 4:30–6:00pm, and again on a Friday or Saturday night. Windows open, then closed, in the bedrooms specifically.
  • Use a phone dB meter for a rough read. Outdoors, ~55 dB is quiet suburban; 65+ dB is arterial.
  • Pull the road's actual traffic volume from the DRCOG regional traffic count map before you go.
  • A 2012 Colorado build has double‑pane windows but not acoustic glass. Laminated acoustic units on the exposed side run $600–1,200 per window — a real and effective fix, and a legitimate thing to ask the seller to credit.

But here's the part that matters for you specifically: studies put the discount on homes near busy roads at roughly 5–12%. Whatever that discount is here, it is in the price you pay and in the price you'll get when you sell — and you're a forced seller on a short clock. If you buy this house, you should be capturing that discount, not paying through it. Ask Karl to show you how the comps he pulls compare on road exposure.

Maintenance — survivable for two years, expensive for five

A 2012 house is at the age where the first wave of replacements arrives. Roof (hail‑shortened in Denver), water heater (10–12 year life), furnace and AC (15–20 years) are all inside the window. The $5,000/year reserve in the table is an average, not a schedule — a roof is $15,000–25,000 in one hit.

Before making any offer, ask for the roof's age and the CLUE claim history on the property. In this neighbourhood that single document tells you more about your future costs than the inspection will.

For two years of living there, this is manageable. For three further years run from Taipei, add $3,000–4,000 a year of what amounts to a remote‑owner tax.
