Revised answer

The rent correction moves this. Here's how far.

I had rent at $2,900. You're paying $3,000 for something considerably smaller, so this house is $3,200–3,400. That single input is worth about $4,700 a year, because it works twice: it raises what renting costs you, and it raises what the house earns when you let it.

At $3,300, owning this house costs $36,600 a year including the interest you give up on the cash — against $39,600 to rent the same thing. Owning is now roughly $3,000 a year cheaper before any appreciation. Under my earlier number it was $1,700 a year more expensive. That is the whole swing.

Denver is forecast at 2–4% a year through 2027. A 1.8% hurdle sits below that band; a 3.3% hurdle sits in the middle of it. So the five‑year hold should beat Treasury bills, and the two‑year buy‑and‑sell is a wager with no edge.

Where this leaves you

The question is no longer "is this house a good buy." It's "will you actually be a landlord for three years?"

Buying only works on the five‑year path, and three of those five years are spent letting this house from Taiwan. If you'd genuinely do that — and mean it in year four when a tenant leaves in February — buying is now the better financial choice, and I'd support it. If you know in your gut you'd rather sell and be done when you leave Denver, the two‑year column is the real analysis, and it says rent.

Answer that honestly and the decision makes itself. Everything below is about making the five‑year path work if you choose it.

The decisive question

Three years as a landlord, eleven time zones away

The five‑year path is where the money is, so be clear‑eyed about what it involves. None of this is exotic. All of it is work you cannot delegate entirely.

  • A Denver rental licence. Required for any let of 30 days or more — four‑year term, $50 fee, and a passing inspection by a certified private inspector you arrange and pay for. The licence number must appear in every advertisement.
  • A US tax return every year. Rent paid to a non‑resident is withheld at 30% of gross by default. The fix is the §871(d) election to be taxed on net income instead — the difference between tax on $42,000 and tax on roughly nothing after depreciation. It requires filing Form 1040NR, every year, on time.
  • About $100,000 frozen at closing when you sell. FIRPTA withholding of 15% of the gross price (10% if the buyer will occupy and the price is under $1M), plus Colorado's 2% non‑resident withholding. Both refundable when you file — many months later.
  • Hail. Colorado premiums are up 61% since 2023, wind and hail carry a separate 1–2% deductible ($6,000–12,000 out of pocket), and insurers increasingly pay actual cash value rather than replacement cost on an older roof.
  • A party wall. It's confirmed a duplex, so one neighbour shares your structure. Shared‑wall disputes, their roof work, their tenants and their insurance all touch you, and they're harder to handle remotely.

Karl as your property manager

Your instinct is right, and it isn't about Karl personally. Three things are worth separating.

The conflict is structural. He earns a commission on the sale, then a management fee on the letting. Both of his income streams point at "buy it." That doesn't make his advice wrong — but it means you shouldn't use him as the check on whether to buy. A fair test: ask him to send you the three closed comps that argue against this price. A good agent will do it without flinching.

The licensing is probably fine, but confirm it. Colorado requires a real estate broker's licence to manage property for someone else for compensation, with client money held in a designated trust account under Commission rule CP‑27 and $1,000,000 of errors‑and‑omissions cover. Karl has a licence. The question is whether Kentwood permits property management under its brokerage and covers it under its E&O policy — many sales‑focused brokerages don't. Ask him directly: "Would you be managing this under Kentwood, with a written management agreement and a brokerage trust account?" If the answer is that he'd do it personally on the side, that's a different and weaker arrangement.

The real risk isn't the fee, it's attention. A sales agent managing a couple of properties as a favour is fine until he has a busy spring, changes brokerage, or moves. Then you're finding a manager from Taipei with a tenant in place. So: get a written agreement with a defined scope, a named maintenance response time, and a termination clause — and identify a backup Denver management company before you leave, not after. Keep 8% in your model regardless. If Karl does it for less, treat that as upside, never as the base case.

Condition

"Just renewed" — and why the inspection timing is normal

The renovation is probably the cheap kind

The listing itself tells you what was done: fresh interior paint, hardwood floors, granite counters, stainless appliances, gas range, subway tile, an island, expanded cabinetry. That is a pre‑sale cosmetic refresh — the work that raises the asking price, not the work that lowers your future costs. It says nothing about the systems that will actually cost you money.

So don't let "just renewed" reduce your maintenance reserve. Ask for a written list with dates and receipts for exactly five things:

  1. Roof — age, and whether it's been replaced on a hail claim. This is the big one in Central Park.
  2. Furnace and air conditioning — a 2012 original is at 14 years against a 15–20 year life.
  3. Water heater — 10–12 year life, so an original is overdue.
  4. Sewer line — whether it's ever been scoped.
  5. The CLUE claim history on the property, which shows every insurance claim filed. In this neighbourhood that single document tells you more than the inspection will.

If roof and HVAC come back genuinely new, drop the reserve to $3,500 and the five‑year break‑even falls to 1.53%. If they're original, keep $5,000 and expect one lumpy year.

Inspection after the offer is standard, not a trap

Karl is describing the normal Colorado process, and it protects you. You go under contract, your earnest money (typically about 1%, so $6,000) goes into escrow with the title company — not to the seller — and then the Inspection Objection deadline runs, usually 7 to 15 days after acceptance. Inside that window you inspect, and you can object or simply terminate. Terminate correctly and on time and your earnest money comes back. In practice the Colorado contract lets you walk during that period for essentially any reason.

Three things to hold firm on:

  • Never waive the inspection to make the offer more attractive. In this market you don't need to.
  • Add a separate roof inspection by a roofing contractor and a sewer scope. A general home inspector looks at the roof from the ground or a ladder; in hail country that isn't enough.
  • Ask for 10–12 days rather than 7, and confirm you can attend by video call if you're still in Taiwan. Have Karl there in person.

Get every notice in writing, and diary the deadline yourself — don't rely on anyone else to track it. Missing it is how buyers lose their earnest money.

Unchanged, and still the highest-value item

Sell your Taiwanese stocks before 31 December 2026

This is worth more than anything you'll win in the price negotiation, and it applies whether or not you buy the house — you're moving the money either way.

Taiwan does not tax individual capital gains on listed domestic shares; you pay the 0.3% transaction tax and nothing more. The United States taxes its residents on worldwide income, including Taiwanese stock gains, at 15–20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax. Critically, there is no step‑up in basis when you arrive: the US measures the gain from your original purchase price, however long ago that was.

Arriving around October 2026 gives you roughly 90 days of US presence this year, below the substantial‑presence threshold. You should be a non‑resident for US tax purposes for all of 2026, with residency beginning in 2027. The window to realise those gains outside the US tax net closes on 31 December 2026.

Two caveats. On an F‑1 or J‑1 you're an "exempt individual" (five years and two years respectively) and stay a non‑resident regardless, so the urgency drops — but confirm rather than assume, particularly with the DHS duration‑of‑status rule changing on 15 September 2026. And check the whole thing with a cross‑border CPA before selling anything. This is the one item here worth paying for advice on.

Moving the money

  • Taiwan raised the individual annual outward remittance cap to US$10 million in October 2024. Your $600,000 sits comfortably inside it.
  • Any single remittance of US$500,000 or more requires the Foreign Exchange Receipts or Payments Declaration Form with documented source and purpose, filed through your bank to the Central Bank. Allow two to four weeks — don't start this under a contract deadline.
  • USD/TWD is around 31.89, with the TWD down 4.2% over twelve months, so you'd be converting at a relatively weak TWD. A 3% move on $600,000 is $18,000; consider staging the conversion across two or three tranches.
The risk nobody mentions

A non‑resident gets a $60,000 US estate tax exemption — not $13 million.

If you own this house and die while still a non‑resident, the US taxes it above $60,000 at rates reaching 40%. On a $620,000 house that's roughly $165,000, and there's no US–Taiwan estate tax treaty to soften it. This matters more on the five‑year path, because you'd still own it after leaving. The cheap fix is a ten‑year term life policy covering the holding period — a few hundred dollars a year for a healthy adult. Buy it in the same month you buy the house.

Still open

The road

One quiet night reported by the agent earning the commission isn't a measurement. It isn't dishonest — it's just not evidence. Test it yourself:

  • Stand there on a weekday from 7:30–8:30am and 4:30–6:00pm, and again on a Friday or Saturday night. Windows open, then closed, in the bedrooms specifically.
  • Use a phone dB meter for a rough read. Outdoors, ~55 dB is quiet suburban; 65+ dB is arterial.
  • Pull the road's actual volume from the DRCOG regional traffic count map before you go.
  • A 2012 Colorado build has double‑pane windows but not acoustic glass. Laminated acoustic units on the exposed side run $600–1,200 per window — an effective fix, and a legitimate thing to ask the seller to credit at the inspection objection.

The part that matters for you: studies put the discount on homes near busy roads at roughly 5–12%. Whatever it is here, it's in the price you pay and the price you'll get. On the five‑year path you're selling into the same discount you bought into — which is fine, provided you bought into it rather than paying through it. That's another thing the closed comps will show, if Karl flags which ones front the road.
