The answer

You asked for less risk or more profit. It delivers neither.

Changing the benchmark from T‑bills to equities changes the verdict, and it should. A house at 3.6% opportunity cost is a different proposition from a house at 6.5%.

Run whole‑package returns — rent saved while you live there, rent earned while you let it, sale proceeds net of the 7% exit — and this house returns about 4.7% a year if Denver appreciates at 3%. Denver's own forecasts are 2–4%, which puts the house between 3.8% and 5.7%.

Goldman's ten‑year base case for the S&P 500 is 6.5%; Vanguard and JPMorgan both sit in the 4–7% range. The ranges overlap. The centres don't.

Assumptions

Every scenario starts with the same $623,000 and pays all housing costs out of it, so end balances are comparable. Purchase $600,000 plus $3,000 closing (cash). Sale costs 7%. Rent $3,300/month growing 3% a year — paid in the rent case, earned in the letting case. Carrying costs $14,900/year: property tax $5,700 (Denver 79.6 mills plus Westerly Creek Metropolitan District 68.5 mills), MCA $700, insurance $3,500, maintenance $5,000, sub‑association assumed at zero. Letting phase nets ~$20,000–22,000/year after 8% management, 5% vacancy and a $1,650 leasing allowance. Depreciation recapture of $13,100 deducted at sale; no income tax on rental profit, since depreciation should shelter it.

Equity returns are modelled as clean compounding, which flatters equities slightly by ignoring dividend withholding — see the vehicle section, where that costs 0.2–0.4 points a year depending on how you hold it. It also ignores sequence risk, which cuts the other way. The full model is in denver‑buy‑rent‑model.py in the repository if you want to change an input.

Your test, second limb

Is the house actually the lower-risk asset?

It feels like it, because you never see a price for it. But volatility you can't observe isn't volatility you don't have. Line the two up on the things that actually determine whether you lose money.

The honest summary: the house has lower measured volatility and higher concentration, illiquidity and operational risk. Swapping a diversified position for a single leveraged‑free asset in one neighbourhood of one city is not a reduction in risk. One fair objection, and it's a real one: 0050 is not diversified either. TSMC is roughly 58% of it. Owning 0050 is closer to owning one company than most people admit, and its 28% annualised return over the past five years is that concentration paying off spectacularly — not a repeatable base rate. If the comparison you actually want is "a safer thing than 0050," the answer isn't a Denver duplex; it's a global equity fund.

The fair case for buying

Where the house wins

I've argued one side hard, so here is the other, honestly.

  • If equities disappoint, the house wins. At a 4% S&P the house only needs 2.23% — inside the forecast. Buying is, in effect, a bet that US equities underperform their consensus over the next five years. That is not a crazy bet at 21× forward earnings.
  • The historical Denver number is much better than the forecast. Case‑Shiller Denver returned 7.01% annualised over the ten years to February 2025. If Denver repeats that, the house is equivalent to equities at about 8.4% and wins comfortably. The forecasts say it won't, but the forecasts have been wrong about Denver before.
  • Sequence risk is real. Equities can be down 30% exactly when you want the money. On a five‑year horizon that isn't a remote scenario.
  • It hedges your actual expenses. For two years your costs are in USD. Owning a USD asset is a natural hedge; holding 0050 while paying Denver rent is a currency mismatch.
  • Inflation. Rents and house prices track inflation more directly than a bond does — though equities do too.

What none of these change: even in the cases where the house wins, it wins while being illiquid, concentrated, and three years of work. That was your point, and it stands.

If you invest instead

The vehicle matters nearly as much as the return

This is the part most people get wrong, and it's worth more than a percentage point a year. As a Taiwanese citizen, how you hold equities determines whether you're taxed at 30%, 15%, 0%, or punitively. And the right answer flips depending on your visa.

Two facts drive it. There is no US–Taiwan tax treaty — the Expedited Double‑Tax Relief Act passed the House but has not been enacted, so US‑source dividends to a Taiwan resident are still withheld at the full 30%. And US estate tax follows domicile, not income‑tax residency: on a two‑year posting you almost certainly stay non‑domiciled, so the $60,000 exemption applies to your US‑situs assets the whole time, even if you become a US income‑tax resident.

If you'll be on F‑1 or J‑1 you stay a non‑resident for your whole stay (five years and two years respectively as an "exempt individual"). An Irish‑domiciled S&P fund is then the clean answer: 15% dividend withholding instead of 30%, no US estate exposure, no US filing. Keeping 0050 is also fine on tax grounds — the objection to it is concentration, not tax.

If you'll be on H‑1B or L‑1 you become a US tax resident from 2027, and both foreign‑domiciled funds turn into PFICs. That makes US‑domiciled VOO the right vehicle for income tax — but it's US‑situs, so you'd carry the same $60,000 estate‑tax exposure the house has. The fix is identical and cheap either way: a ten‑year term life policy sized to the exposure.

Note what this does to the comparison. If you'd hold VOO as a non‑resident, the 30% dividend withholding costs roughly 0.36 points a year, trimming the S&P's edge from 1.8 points to about 1.4. Via an Irish fund the drag is about 0.18 points. Either way the ranking doesn't change — but it's real money, and it's free to get right.

Deadline

31 December 2026 now matters twice over

I flagged this before as the highest‑value item. Deciding to stay invested rather than buy makes it more urgent, not less, because it now does two jobs.

One: the gains. Taiwan doesn't tax individual capital gains on listed domestic shares — you pay the 0.3% transaction tax and nothing more. The US taxes residents on worldwide income at 15–20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax, measured from your original cost basis with no step‑up on arrival. Arriving around October gives you ~90 days of US presence in 2026, below the substantial‑presence threshold, so you should be a non‑resident for all of 2026 with residency beginning in 2027.

Two: the PFIC problem. If you're heading for H‑1B or L‑1, every share of 0050 you still hold on 1 January 2027 becomes a PFIC in your hands. Selling before year end solves the capital gains question and the PFIC question in a single transaction — and you can rebuy the same exposure through a US‑domiciled fund the following week.

On F‑1 or J‑1 neither problem bites, because you remain a non‑resident. Which is precisely why the visa is the one thing I still need from you — it determines whether this deadline is worth five figures or nothing at all.

Either way: confirm with a US–Taiwan cross‑border CPA before you sell anything. This is the one item here worth paying for advice on.

Moving money, if you do buy

  • Taiwan's individual annual outward remittance cap rose to US$10 million in October 2024 — $600,000 sits well inside it.
  • Any single remittance of US$500,000 or more needs the Foreign Exchange Receipts or Payments Declaration Form with documented source and purpose, filed through your bank to the Central Bank. Allow two to four weeks; don't start it under a contract deadline.
  • USD/TWD is around 31.89, the TWD down 4.2% over twelve months. A 3% move on $600,000 is $18,000 — stage the conversion across two or three tranches.
If you buy anyway

Then at least buy it properly

The financial case is weaker than equities, but it isn't absurd, and there are non‑financial reasons to own where you live. If you go ahead, these are the things that actually move the outcome.

Price

Forty‑nine days on market against a ~40‑day median for attached homes, one cut of 2.4% already, and a metro where 62.9% of closings include a concession (median $10,000, with buyers extracting $15,000–50,000 of total value) against a 99.1% close‑to‑list ratio that excludes every one of them. A realistic landing zone is $595,000–605,000 plus $10,000–15,000 in concessions. Open at $580,000.

But look at the break‑even table before you spend energy here: landing $585,000 instead of $600,000 moves the five‑year hurdle from 4.96% to 4.78%. Almost nothing. Negotiating only makes money if you buy below market — so the thing to ask Karl for is closed comps: Central Park paired homes, 1,500–1,800 sq ft, last 90 days. One correction from earlier: don't tell the seller his rental fallback is weak. At $3,300 he'd gross 6.4% and net near 3.9%. He can afford to wait.

Condition

"Just renewed" describes what the listing describes: paint, hardwood, granite, appliances, subway tile, cabinetry. That's a pre‑sale cosmetic refresh — the work that raises the asking price, not the work that lowers your costs. Don't reduce the maintenance reserve for it. Ask for dated receipts on five things: roof (and whether it was a hail claim), furnace, air conditioning, water heater, sewer line — plus the CLUE claim history, which lists every insurance claim ever filed on the property.

Inspection after the offer is normal Colorado process and it protects you. Earnest money (~1%, so $6,000) goes into the title company's escrow, not to the seller, and the Inspection Objection deadline runs 7–15 days after acceptance. Terminate correctly and on time and it comes back; in practice you can walk during that window for essentially any reason. Never waive it, ask for 10–12 days rather than 7, and add a roofer's inspection and a sewer scope on top of the general one. Diary the deadline yourself.

The road, and the estate-tax trap

One quiet night reported by the agent earning the commission isn't a measurement. Stand there on a weekday at 7:30–8:30am and 4:30–6:00pm and again on a weekend night, windows open then closed, in the bedrooms; use a phone dB meter (~55 dB outdoors is quiet suburban, 65+ is arterial); and pull the road's volume from the DRCOG traffic count map first. A 2012 build has double‑pane but not acoustic glass — laminated units run $600–1,200 per window and are a legitimate inspection‑objection credit. Busy‑road discounts run 5–12% in the literature, and that discount is in what you pay and what you'll get. Tell me the street name and I'll pull the real counts.

And whatever you decide: if you end up holding US‑situs assets — this house, or a US‑domiciled ETF — buy a ten‑year term life policy sized to the exposure. A $620,000 house above a $60,000 exemption is roughly $165,000 of US estate tax with no treaty to soften it. The insurance costs a few hundred dollars a year.
